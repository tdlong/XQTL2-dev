Much of the genome contributes to haplotype change in long lived individuals. Each sex × diet group was scanned independently, contrasting the longest-lived 3–11% of each of 10 replicates per treatment with an contemporaneus unselected control individuals (Fig. 1a). Dividing the genetic map into 268 not wholly heterochromatic 1 cM tiles at a -log10(p) Wald (LWP) threshold of 7.5, 32% of tiles are differentiated in at least one treatment and 12% tiles at a threshold of 15. The mapping population is an advanced- rather than a single-generation cross, and as a result unrecombined founder haplotypes are broken into small blocks and the strongest peaks are correspondingly narrow. We defined eight and ten peaks with local maximum LWP exceeding 15 or 7.5 respectively greater than 5cM apart from one another (Table S1). Constructing 2 LWP support intervals for each of the 18 peaks results in an 80% confidence interval on width of 0.22 to 1.18 cM (140 kb to 775 kb).
Longevity is highly polygenic. Wald scores establish that a region shows significant changes in founder haplotype frequencies, but do not estimate the magnitude of each region's contribution, and given the sample size small haplotype frequency shifts can be highly significant. The squared changes in founder frequency per window as a function of selected proportions can be used to estimate the heritability explained for each window, which places the scan on a scale of phenotypic variance (Fig. 1b). Both LWP and heritability measures derive from the same frequency changes, they are tightly related (r = 0.98), and heritability can be expressed as a simple function of the number of chromosomes in the sequenced sample and the proportion of individuals in the truncated tail. This function predicts heritabilities of 0.14%, 0.37% and 0.65% at LWP thresholds of 2, 7.5 and 15 repectively. Despite 0.4% being a subtle heritability estimate at the scale of the experiment we carried out, heritabilities this small can be resolved, and a substantial fraction of the genome is associated with these subtle fractions of variation. This quantification required screening ~114 thousand flies, selecting the longest-lived ~5%, and sequencing on average 5.7 thousand selected and 6.9 thousand control individuals per treatment. Regions contributing less than 2% to heritable variation would have gone undetected in prior work employing modestly sized mapping panels consisting of several hundred RILs (cites), or association type panels consisting of 100s of ILs or 1000s of genotyped individuals. Nonetheless this pattern of variation in heritability throughout the genome is consistent with work attempting to reconcile GWAS hits and affected sib linkage mapping results for human height and BMI (Sidenko), in which regional variation in LOD scores are largely consistent with the cumulative effects of underlying polygenes known from large scale GWAS studies.
Architecture differs between the sexes but is largely invariant to diet. The X chromosome is 24% of the genetic map, but only 3% of its 1cM tiles exceed 7.5 and none exceeds 15, compared to 41% and 16% for the autosomes. One explanation is hemizygosity, partially recessive alleles are exposed to purifying selection every generation in males and standing variation is depleted. On the autosomes 83 of 203 tiles exceed a LWP of 7.5 in at least one of the four treatments. Averaging heritability over the two diets within each sex, males are the highest at 52% of those tiles, so neither sex is systematically more responsive in our experiment. We wished to partition the patterns of heritability in the four treatments into a main effect, a source due to sex, and a source due to diet. We split the ten replicate contrasts per treatment into sets of five, obtained independent estimates of heritability from each sub-set and partititoned the heritability (Fig. 1c), after subtracting the pure error componet the subsetting allowed us to estimate. Over the tiles with LWP exceeding 7.5, 13.2% (95% CI 10.0-19.1) of the variation among the four groups is sex-specific and 1.8% (1.1-3.6) diet-specific, with no detectable interaction (-0.4%, -0.8 to 0.1). But it is apparent from Fig. 1c that the relative partitioning of variation varies as a function of location. This regional variation in genotype by environmental interaction would be refractory to analyses that treat sex and/or environmental variables as covariates removed from phenotypic values before estimating polygenic effects. This would typically be the case in human genetics where the environment is not experimentally manipulated and can vary geographically with genotype. Although longevity in Drosophila under two sugar diets is only a single example, if this observation is general it may have implications for polygenic risk scores.
A large-effect loci sits on top of the polygenic background. The peak on chromosome 3L at 9.30 Mb reaches a LWP of 163.8 in high-sugar males and exceeds 36 in all four groups, in a genome where only 12% of tiles exceed 15 in any group. It accounts for 4.7% of phenotypic variance in high-sugar males and 1.5% in low-sugar females, two to three times the largest of the other regions we detect. It is the most tightly localised of the peaks in Table S1, with a two LWP support interval of 55 kb or 0.22 cM. For this region the change in founder haplotype frequencies is dominated by founder B3 (Fig. 2d), whose frequency decrease in selected pools ranges from 6.3% in low sugar females to 10.3% in high sugar males. This is a much larger allele frequency change that is observed at any other peaks. There is no evidence that the haplotype at B3 is unconditionally deleterious as its change in frequency over time in the synthetic population we maintain is typical of other regions and founders (Fig. 2H). B3 represents a naturally occurring allele harboring a factor or factors contributing to poor representation in long lived individuals in both sexes and diets. The interval contains the upstream regulatory region of peptidoglycan recognition protein (PGRP-LA), part of a gene complex that is a component of innate immune signaling in Drosophila, a gene not previously identified as a candidate-gene nor in intervention studies of longevity.
