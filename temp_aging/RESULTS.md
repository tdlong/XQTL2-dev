Much of the genome contributes to haplotype change in long lived individuals. Each sex × diet group was scanned independently, contrasting the longest-lived 3–11% of each of 10 replicates per treatment with contemporaneous unselected control individuals (Fig. 1a). Dividing the genetic map into 268 not wholly heterochromatic 1 cM tiles at a -log10(p) Wald (LWP) threshold of 7.5, 32% of tiles are differentiated in at least one treatment and 12% tiles at a threshold of 15. The mapping population is an advanced- rather than a single-generation cross, and as a result unrecombined founder haplotypes are broken into small blocks and the strongest peaks are correspondingly narrow. We defined eight and ten peaks with local maximum LWP exceeding 15 or 7.5 respectively greater than 5cM apart from one another (Table S1). Constructing 2 LWP support intervals for each of the 18 peaks results in an 80% range on width of 0.25 to 1.51 cM, and of 168 to 828 kb.

Wald scores establish that a region shows significant changes in founder haplotype frequencies, but do not estimate the magnitude of each region's contribution, and small haplotype frequency shifts can be highly significant. The squared changes in founder frequency per window as a function of selected proportions can be used to estimate the heritability explained for each window, which places the scan on a scale of phenotypic variance (Fig. 1b). LWP and heritability measures are tightly related (r = 0.98) and heritability can be expressed as a simple function of the number of chromosomes in the sequenced sample and the proportion of individuals in the truncated tail, predicting heritabilities of 0.09%, 0.32% and 0.61% at LWP thresholds of 2, 7.5 and 15 respectively. Despite 0.32% being a subtle heritability estimate of experiment can resolve heritabilities this small and a substantial fraction of the genome is associated with these subtle fractions of variation. This detection limit required screening ~114 thousand flies, selecting the longest-lived ~5%, and sequencing several thousand selected and control individuals per treatment. Regions contributing less than 2% to heritable variation would have gone undetected in prior work employing modestly sized mapping panels consisting of several hundred RILs (cites), or association type panels consisting of 100s of ILs or 1000s of genotyped individuals (Supplementary Result X). The patterns of variation in heritability we observe throughout the genome is consistent with work attempting to reconcile GWAS hits and affected sib linkage mapping results for human height and BMI (Sidenko), where the authors argue that linkage-based LOD scores are largely consistent with the cumulative effects of underlying polygenes known from large scale GWAS studies.

The X chromosome is depauperate in detected peaks: despite being 24% of the genetic map, only 3% of its 1 cM tiles exceed 7.5 and none exceeds 15, compared to 41% and 16% for the autosomes. Perhaps standing genetic variation is depleted because partially recessive alleles are exposed to purifying selection every generation in hemizygous males. On the autosomes 83 of 203 tiles exceed a LWP of 7.5 in at least one of the four treatments. Averaging heritability over the two diets within each sex, male peaks are the highest at 52% of those tiles, so neither sex is systematically more responsive in our experiment. We partitioned the heritability across the four treatments into a main effect, a source due to sex, and a source due to diet (Fig. 1c). Over the tiles with LWP exceeding 7.5, 13.2% of the variation among the four groups is sex-specific and 1.8% diet-specific. It is also apparent from Fig. 1c that the relative partitioning of variation varies as a function of genomic location. This regional variation in genotype by environmental interaction would be refractory to analyses that treat sex and/or environmental variables as covariates removed from phenotypic values before estimating polygenic effects. This would typically be the case in human genetics where the environment is not experimentally manipulated and can vary geographically with genotype. Although longevity in Drosophila under two sugar diets is only a single example, if this observation is general it may have implications for polygenic risk scores.

A large-effect locus sits on top of the polygenic background. The peak on chromosome 3L at 9.30 Mb reaches a LWP of 163.8 in high-sugar males and exceeds 36 in all four groups, in a genome where only 12% of tiles exceed 15 in any group. It accounts for 4.7% of phenotypic variance in high-sugar males and 1.5% in low-sugar females, two to three times the largest of the other regions we detect. It has the narrowest support interval in physical terms of any peak in Table S1 at 55 kb. For this region the change in founder haplotype frequencies is dominated by founder B3 (Fig. 2d), whose frequency decrease in selected pools ranges from 6.3% in low sugar females to 10.3% in high sugar males. This is a much larger allele frequency change than is observed at any other peak. There is no evidence that the haplotype at B3 is unconditionally deleterious as its change in frequency over time in the synthetic population we maintain is typical of other regions and founders (Fig. 2H). B3 represents a naturally occurring allele harboring a factor or factors contributing to poor representation in long lived individuals in both sexes and diets. The mapped interval contains the upstream regulatory region of peptidoglycan recognition protein (PGRP-LA), part of a gene complex that is a component of innate immune signaling in Drosophila, a gene not previously identified as a candidate-gene nor in intervention studies of longevity.
