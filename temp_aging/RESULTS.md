# Results

Much of the genome contributes to haplotype change in long lived individuals. Each sex × diet group was scanned independently, contrasting the longest-lived 3–11% of each of 10 replicates per treatment with an unselected sample from the same cage ("same cage" not exactly how it was done, but can be described in detail in M&M) (Fig. 1a). Dividing the genetic map into 268 not wholly heterochromatic 1 cM tiles at a -log10(p) Wald threshold of 7.5, 32% of tiles respond in at least one group and 12% exceed a threshold of 15. Because the mapping population is an advanced- rather than a single-generation cross, founder haplotypes are broken into short blocks and the stronger peaks are correspondingly narrow. We defined eight and ten peaks with local maximum -log10 P values exceeding 15 or 7.5 respectively greater than 5cM apart from one another (Table S1). Constructing 2 -log10 P value support intervals for each of the 18 peaks results in an 80% confidence interval on width of 0.22 to 1.18 cM (140 kb to 775 kb).

Longevity is highly polygenic. Wald scores establish that a region shows significant changes in founder haplotype frequencies, but do not estimate the magnitude of each region's contribution, and given the sample size small haplotype frequency shifts can be highly significant. Because the population derives from eight inbred founders and the selected proportion for each cage is known, the squared changes in founder frequency per window can be used to estimate the heritability explained by that window (Methods), which places the scan on a scale of phenotypic variance (Fig. 1b). Heritability estimates are necessarily positive, so one cM tiles whose Wald score never exceeds 2 define a heritability floor of 0.07%. Although significant tiles at a -log10 P of 7.5 average heritabilities of 0.38%, and highly significant tiles at 15 average 0.68%. Since 32% of tiles exceed 7.5 and 12% exceed 15, a substantial fraction of the genome are associated with these small but significant heritability estimates of a few tenths of a per cent of the phenotypic variance. In order to quantify these subtle contributions to variation in longevity we screen on the order of one hundred thousand flies per treatment, selecting the longest-lived ~5% and sequencing ~23 thousand selected and ~14 thousand control individuals. These subtle effect loci would have gone undetected in prior work employing modestly sized mapping panels consisting of several hundred RILs (cites), or association type panels consisting of 100s of ILs or 1000s of genotyped individuals. But this observation is consistent with work attempting to reconcile GWAS hits and affected sib linkage mapping results for human height and BMI (Sidenko), in which regional variation in LOD scores are largely consistent with the cumulative effects of underlying polygenes known from large scale GWAS studies.

Architecture differs between the sexes but is largely invariant to diet. Of the 268 one cM tiles, 85 reach a -log10 P of 7.5 in at least one group. Male heritability exceeds female at both of the two significant tiles on the X. On the autosomes neither sex is systematically higher, males exceeding females at 52% of 83 tiles. This is perhaps of interest as the X chromosome is hemizygous in Drosophila males. We split the ten replicate contrasts per treatment into sets of five to obtain two independent estimates of heritability at each tile and partitioned the heritability into a component shared by all four groups, components differing by sex or by diet (Fig. 1c), after subtracting the pure error componet. Over the significant tiles 13.2% (95% CI 10.0-19.1) of the variation among the four groups is sex-specific and 1.8% (1.1-3.6) diet-specific, with no detectable interaction (-0.4%, -0.8 to 0.1); over the autosomes alone the sex-specific fraction is 13.1% (9.9-19.3). But it is apparent from Fig. 1c that the relative partitioning of variation varies as a function of location. This genotype by environmental interaction would be refractory to analyses that treat sex and/or environmental variables as covariates removed from phenotypic values before estimating polygenic effects. This would typically be the case in human genetics where the environment is not manipulated and can vary geographically as does genotype. Although longevity in Drosophila under two sugar diets is only a single example, if this observation is general it may have implications for polygenic risk scores.

A large-effect loci sits on top of the polygenic background. The peak on chromosome 3L at 9.30 Mb reaches a -log10 P of 163.8 in high-sugar males and exceeds 36 in all four groups, in a genome where only 12% of tiles exceed 15 in any group. It accounts for 4.7% of phenotypic variance in high-sugar males and 1.5% in low-sugar females, two to three times the largest of the other regions we detect. It is the most tightly localised of the peaks in Table S1, with a two -log10 P support interval of 55 kb or 0.22 cM. For this region the change in founder haplotype frequencies is dominated by founder B3 (Fig. 2d), whose frequency decrease in selected pools ranges from 6.3% in low sugar females to 10.3% in high sugar males. This is a much larger allele frequency change that is observed at any other peaks. There is no evidence that the haplotype at B3 is unconditionally deleterious as its change in frequency over time in the synthetic population we maintain is typical of other regions and founders (Fig. 2H). B3 represents a naturally occurring allele harboring a factor or factors contributing to poor representation in long lived individuals in both sexes and diets. The interval contains the upstream regulatory region of peptidoglycan recognition protein (PGRP-LA), part of a gene complex that is a component of innate immune signaling in Drosophila, a gene not previously identified as a candidate-gene nor in intervention studies of longevity.
