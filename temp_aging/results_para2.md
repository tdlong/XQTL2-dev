Wald scores establish that a region shows significant changes in founder haplotype frequencies, but do not estimate the magnitude of each region's contribution, and given the sample size small haplotype frequency shifts can be highly significant. Because the population derives from eight inbred founders and the selected proportion for each cage is known, the squared changes in founder frequency per window can be used to estimate the heritability explained by that window (Methods), which places the scan on a scale of phenotypic variance (Fig. 1b). Heritability estimates are necessarily positive, so a tile at which nothing has happened still returns a value: averaged over the four traits, 1 cM tiles whose Wald score never exceeds 2 sit at 0.68% and define a floor. Fitting tile heritability against tile Wald score and reading off the same two cutoffs used above, significant tiles at a -log10 P of 7.5 average 1.00%, and highly significant tiles at 15 average 1.31%. Since 37% of tiles exceed 7.5 and 12% exceed 15, a substantial fraction of the genome carries changes of this magnitude, each explaining on the order of 1% of phenotypic variance against a floor of 0.68%. The highly significant chr3L locus reaches 4.5% of phenotypic variance in high-sugar males and 2.2% in low-sugar females, but the next largest regions although highly significant only reach h^2 values of 1.5–2.3%. Detecting effects of this size required screening ~104,000 flies per treatment, selecting the longest-lived 5.2% and sequencing 22,896 selected individuals. Even if each of these regions were explained by a single causative locus, a locus accounting for 1–2% of phenotypic variance would likely have gone undetected in the modestly sized mapping panels of several hundred RILs (cites), or association type panels consisting of 100s of ILs or 1000s of genotyped individuals. In aggregate, however, they account for a substantial fraction of the heritability of longevity. It is striking that variation throughout the genome in the -log10 P Wald scores are congruent with attempts to reconcile GWAS and affected sib linkage mapping results in humans (Sidenko).
