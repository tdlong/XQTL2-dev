Longevity is highly polygenic. Wald scores establish that a region shows significant changes in founder haplotype frequencies, but do not estimate the magnitude of each region's contribution, and given the sample size small shifts can be highly significant. Because the population derives from eight inbred founders and the selected proportion for each cage is known, the squared changes in founder frequency per window provides an estimate of the heritability explained by that window (Methods), placing the scan on a scale of phenotypic variance (Fig. 1b). We plotted per window heritability estimates against -log10 P Wald scores per trait and established that heritability estimates of 0.80–0.88% correspond to a Wald threshold of 5. Between 29% and 49% of tiles exceed that value, depending on the trait. Individual contributions are nonetheless small. The chr3L locus reaches 4.5% of phenotypic variance in high-sugar males and 2.2% in low-sugar females; the next largest regions reach 1.5–2.3%, and the median tile carries 1.0% with three quarters below 1.3%. Resolving contributions of this magnitude required screening ~104,000 flies per treatment, selecting the longest-lived 5.2% and sequencing 22,896 selected individuals. Although these regions represent substantial fractions of heritability for longevity, even if each local peak were explained by a single causative locus it would likely have gone undetected in the modestly sized mapping panels of several hundred RILs (cites), or association type panels consisting of 100s of ILs or 1000s of genotyped individuals. It is striking that variation throughout the genome in the -log10 P Wald scores are congruent with attempts to reconcile GWAS and affected sib linkage mapping results in humans (Sidenko).
