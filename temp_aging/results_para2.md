Longevity is highly polygenic. Wald scores establish that a region shows significant changes in founder haplotype frequencies, but do not estimate the magnitude of each region's contribution, and given the sample size small haplotype frequency shifts can be highly significant. Because the population derives from eight inbred founders and the selected proportion for each cage is known, the squared changes in founder frequency per window can be used to estimate the heritability explained by that window (Methods), which places the scan on a scale of phenotypic variance (Fig. 1b). Heritability estimates are necessarily positive, so one cM tiles whose Wald score never exceeds 2 define a heritability floor of 0.68%. Although significant tiles at a -log10 P of 7.5 average heritabilities of 1.00%, and highly significant tiles at 15 average 1.31%. Since 37% of tiles exceed 7.5 and 12% exceed 15, a substantial fraction of the genome are associated with these small but significant heritability estimates of slightly more than 1% of the phenotypic variance. In order to quantify these subtle contributions to variation in longevity we screen on the order of one hundred thousand flies per treatment, selecting the longest-lived ~5% and sequencing ~25 thousand selected and control individuals. These subtle effect loci would have gone undetected in prior work employing modestly sized mapping panels consisting of several hundred RILs (cites), or association type panels consisting of 100s of ILs or 1000s of genotyped individuals. But this observation is consistent with work attempting to reconcile GWAS hits and affected sib linkage mapping results for human height and BMI (Sidenko), in which regional variation in LOD scores are largely consistent with the cumulative effects of underlying polygenes known from large scale GWAS studies.
