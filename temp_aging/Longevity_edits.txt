1. REPLACE the intro paragraph beginning 'Lifespan is mechanistically conserved'
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Lifespan is mechanistically conserved across metazoans (11336665 - Kenyon insulin review; 18340043 - delete worm aging genes in yeast and find that they more often than not impact yeast aging; 17411345 - Ruvkun finds essential worm genes that also extend lifespan and show they are conserved to humans; 28007498 - oldish DR conservation review), varies among individuals of a species, and the variation is heritable (27485207 - my DSPR RIL mapping; 40326784 - DO mouse mapping; 42020758 - Rob williams 4-way mouse mapping population; 41353149 - Trudy AIP from DGRP; 32134916 - Trudy GWAS with DGRP). In Drosophila melanogaster narrow-sense estimates run from under 10% in selection experiments to 31–47% in an outbred population derived from the same founders used here (30356225), the difference reflecting the fertility cost that confounds selection on late life. Longevity in flies is polygenic, fitness-adjacent, and measurable in large cohorts under standardized conditions. Candidate-gene and pathway studies have identified mutations, diets and drugs that extend lifespan, but efforts to characterize the standing variation that controls it, or its interaction with diet and drugs, have produced inconsistent results. One explanation is that longevity is highly polygenic in the manner of human height or BMI; another is a few genes of large to intermediate effect against a polygenic background; experiments to date have not been large enough to tell these apart. Here we use extreme QTL (X-QTL) mapping in an eight-founder advanced-intercross synthetic population, phenotyping >410,000 flies across both sexes and two dietary yeast levels in ten replicate experiments. Heritable signal is elevated across essentially the entire euchromatic genome in every sex-by-diet combination, partitions into a shared effect plus sex and diet components, and is organized in regional blocks rather than uniformly. One locus rises above the polygenic background and accounts for 2–5% of lifespan variation. Natural variation in lifespan is not concentrated in a set of aging genes; it is spread across the genome in a sex- and diet-dependent manner, and nine replicate experiments predict the tenth.

3. REPLACE the paragraph beginning 'Linkage based mapping signals'
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Linkage-based mapping signals integrate polygenic architecture locally. In humans, affected-sib-pair linkage signals for height and body mass index can be reconstructed by summing GWAS effects over chromosomal segments (Sidorenko). A multiparent advanced intercross performs that integration directly: each founder haplotype block carries whatever causal variants segregate within it, and the shift in its frequency between control and selected pools measures the block's aggregate effect. The mapping signal is therefore a readout of polygenic architecture along the genome. Longevity suits this design because the phenotypic tail selects itself — a large cohort is set up, and the few flies still alive at the end are the extreme — and pooled sequencing replaces individual genotyping. The combination lets us query hundreds of thousands of flies per sex and diet, and replicate at the scale needed to detect and quantify subtle linkage signals.

4. REPLACE the paragraph beginning 'Our implementation follows earlier XQTL'
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Our implementation follows earlier X-QTL experiments in Drosophila [Macdonald and Long refs]. The mapping population is a synthetic population founded by intercrossing >600 recombinant inbred lines of the DSPR and maintained continuously since, six media bottles added each week and removed after three. All lines descend from eight inbred founder strains with reference-quality genome assemblies (King Genetics, Genome Res, Mahul's paper), and the population has recombined for [XX] generations, so founder haplotypes persist only as short blocks. We seeded each aging cage with roughly 10,000 flies of one sex on a sugar-yeast medium containing 10% (SY10) or 20% yeast (SY20), SY10 being a restricted diet that extends lifespan in this population (Supplementary Fig. ?), held cages at 25 °C, replaced food every two days, and harvested the last ~500 flies alive after 48–73 days depending on sex, diet and replicate. Ten replicates per sex-by-diet treatment ran over 18 months, each replicate contributing one unselected control pool per sex drawn from the same cohort at its start, so that one control serves both diets. We sequenced pooled DNA from each survivor pool and its control to 50–200×, counted reads at SNPs differentially fixed among the founders, and estimated founder haplotype frequencies in overlapping windows genome-wide (Fig. 1a). The contrast between survivor and control pools is the mapping signal. Because the population is an advanced rather than a single-generation cross, each 1 cM tile reports the aggregate effect of a short haplotype block rather than a whole chromosome arm.

6. REPLACE the Results paragraph beginning 'Much of the genome contributes'
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Much of the genome contributes to haplotype change in long-lived individuals. Each sex × diet group was scanned independently, contrasting the longest-lived 3–11% of each of ten replicates with the unselected controls (Fig. 1a). Dividing the genetic map into 268 one-cM tiles that are not wholly heterochromatic, and scoring a tile by its largest -log10 P Wald statistic (LWP) in any treatment, 32% of tiles exceed 7.5 and 12% exceed 15. Because the population is an advanced cross, unrecombined founder haplotypes are short and the strongest peaks are correspondingly narrow: we define eight peaks with local maximum LWP above 15 and ten more above 7.5, each at least 5 cM from any stronger peak (Table S1), and two-LWP support intervals on the eighteen have an 80% range in width of 0.25–1.51 cM, or 168–828 kb.

8. REPLACE the paragraph beginning 'Wald scores establish'
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Wald scores establish that a region's founder frequencies change, not by how much, and small shifts can be highly significant. The squared change in founder frequency per window, scaled by the selected proportion, estimates the heritability each window explains and places the scan on the scale of phenotypic variance (Fig. 1b). LWP and heritability are tightly related (r = 0.98), and heritability is a simple function of the number of chromosomes sequenced and the proportion selected, so LWP thresholds of 2, 7.5 and 15 correspond to heritabilities of 0.09%, 0.32% and 0.61%. The experiment resolves heritabilities as small as 0.3%, and a third of the genome is associated with variation at that scale. Reaching this limit required screening ~104,000 flies per treatment, selecting the longest-lived ~5%, and sequencing several thousand selected and control flies per treatment. Regions contributing less than 2% of heritable variation would have gone undetected in prior work with mapping panels of several hundred RILs (27485207) or association panels of hundreds of inbred lines or thousands of genotyped individuals (Supplementary Result X). The pattern recalls the reconciliation of GWAS and affected-sib linkage results for human height and BMI (Sidorenko), where linkage LOD scores are consistent with the cumulative effects of polygenes known from large GWAS.

10. REPLACE the paragraph beginning 'The X chromosome is depauperate'
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The X chromosome is depauperate in peaks: it is 24% of the genetic map, yet only 3% of its 1 cM tiles exceed LWP 7.5 and none exceeds 15, against 41% and 16% for the autosomes, perhaps because partially recessive alleles are exposed to purifying selection every generation in hemizygous males. On the autosomes 83 of 203 tiles exceed 7.5 in at least one treatment. Averaging heritability over diets within sex, male peaks are the higher at 52% of those tiles, so neither sex is systematically more responsive. Partitioning heritability across the four treatments into a shared component and components due to sex and to diet (Fig. 1c), 13.2% of the variation among treatments over tiles above 7.5 is sex-specific and 1.8% diet-specific, and the partition varies with genomic location: the strongest peaks are detected in all four treatments, while weaker signal is more often sex- or diet-specific. Regional genotype-by-environment interaction of this kind would be invisible to analyses that remove sex or environment as covariates before estimating polygenic effects, the usual practice in human genetics, where environment is not manipulated and may covary geographically with genotype. Longevity under two diets is a single example, but if the observation is general it bears on polygenic risk scores.

11. REPLACE the paragraph beginning 'A large-effect locus sits on top'
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A large-effect locus sits on top of the polygenic background. The peak on chromosome 3L at 9.30 Mb reaches an LWP of 163.8 in high-sugar males and exceeds 36 in all four groups, in a genome where only 12% of tiles exceed 15 in any group. It accounts for 4.7% of phenotypic variance in high-sugar males and 1.5% in low-sugar females, two to three times the largest of the other regions we detect, and has the narrowest support interval in physical terms of any peak in Table S1, 55 kb. The change in founder frequencies here is dominated by founder B3 (Fig. 2d), which falls by 6.3% in low-sugar females to 10.3% in high-sugar males in the selected pools, a far larger shift than at any other peak. The B3 haplotype is not unconditionally deleterious: its frequency over time in the synthetic population is typical of other regions and founders (Fig. 2h). B3 carries a naturally occurring allele, or alleles, that reduce representation among long-lived flies in both sexes and on both diets. The mapped interval contains the upstream regulatory region of peptidoglycan recognition protein LA (PGRP-LA), part of a gene complex in innate immune signalling, a gene not previously identified as a candidate for longevity nor in intervention studies.

12. ADD as a NEW Results paragraph directly after it
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We can predict heritable variation in longevity in held-out replicates. Holding out each of the ten replicates in turn, founder-frequency changes at the *N* best markers in the other nine were used to predict the held-out replicate. For the ten best markers the prediction has *R*² = 0.60–0.69 across treatments (Fig. Ya). Marker-based heritability for the whole genome is 11–17% across treatments (SE 0.7–1.2; Methods), of which tiles that never reach an LWP of 7.5 carry 40–47%. A predictor built on the ten best markers explains 6.4–9.5% of phenotypic variance and one built on thirty explains 8.2–11.6% (Fig. Yb), or 66–72% of the marker-based heritability. That is, thirty haplotype blocks chosen in nine experiments account for most of the heritability detectable in a tenth. Despite variation in longevity being polygenic and spread throughout the genome, a few dozen segments explain a large proportion of heritable variation.

**Marker-based heritability.** The heritability of each trait that founder haplotypes capture genome-wide is the sum of $\hat{h}^2$ over the 268 1 cM tiles, corrected for one locus changing founder frequencies at every tile linked to it and so being counted more than once. The number of tiles a locus is counted in is the integral of its footprint, $\hat{h}^2$ against distance, divided by its height. Over the peaks of Table S1, each in its strongest treatment and on the side free of another peak within 6 cM, $\hat{h}^2$ falls to a tenth of the peak by 2–6 cM and the footprint integral is 3.8 tiles pooled over peaks (median 3.4; 4.1 at 3L 9.30). Marker-based heritability is therefore

$$
\frac{1}{3.8}\sum_{\mathrm{tiles}} \hat{h}^2 ,
$$

and is 11.4, 17.1, 13.4 and 16.4% for SY10 females, SY10 males, SY20 females and SY20 males, with jackknife standard errors over replicates of 0.7–1.2. It is the counterpart of SNP-based heritability at the resolution of founder haplotype blocks, and like LD score regression assumes one correction factor along the genome. The eighteen peaks of Table S1 sum to 72–80% of it, an in-sample upper bound on what peaks explain.

**Out-of-sample prediction.** We asked if changes in founder haplotype frequencies at leading factors can predict an unseen experiment in a manner analogous to polygenic risk scores. Each of the ten independent replicates was held out in turn and the other nine were used to build the predictor; we write ⓡ for the held-out replicate. From the other nine we estimated founder frequency changes per unit of selection, $e_{fr} = \Delta p_{fr}/i_r$ as in the heritability estimate above, and their mean $\bar{e}_f$ (the bar denotes the average over the nine non-held-out replicates in the current context); ranked tiles by heritability explained; and kept the strongest predictors separated by at least 5 cM. The predicted change in founder frequency in the held-out replicate at a given location is its known selection intensity times that mean,

$$
\widehat{\Delta p}_{fⓡ} = i_{ⓡ}\, \bar{e}_f .
$$

Heritability explained in the held-out replicate at a tile is by extension

$$
\hat{h}^2_{ⓡ} = 100\,k \sum_f \frac{\bar{e}_f\, e_{fⓡ}}{\hat{p}_f} .
$$

The $s_f^2/R$ term of the earlier heritability estimator does not appear, as $\bar{e}_f$ and $e_{fⓡ}$ are independent estimates of the same quantity, so their product has expectation $\bar{e}_f^{\,2}$. The tiles of a predictor are at least 5 cM apart, where the correlation between tiles is 0.25–0.34, and their heritabilities are added without correction; dividing out the $N \times N$ correlation among them changes the sums by at most 5%. The heritability a predictor built on $N$ tiles explains in the held-out replicate (Fig. Yb) is the sum of $\hat{h}^2_{ⓡ}$ over its tiles multiplied by the ratio of that sum to the same sum with $e_{fⓡ}$ replaced by $\bar{e}_f$: the fraction of the training signal that reproduces, which is the reliability of the predictor's weights. The dashed line in Fig. Yb is the marker-based heritability defined above.
