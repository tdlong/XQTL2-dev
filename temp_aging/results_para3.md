Architecture differs between the sexes but is largely invariant to diet. Restricting to the 98 of 268 tiles that reach a -log10 P of 7.5 in at least one group, heritability is sharply sex-dependent. Male heritability exceeds female at every one of the 15 significant tiles on the X, at 78% of the 18 tiles on 2L and 78% of the 18 on 3L, with median heritabilities of 1.36% against 0.63%, 1.19% against 0.70% and 1.71% against 1.26%; whereas on 3R the direction reverses and females exceed males at 90% of 21 tiles (0.93% against 0.71%), and 2R shows no consistent difference. The X chromosome is hemizygous in Drosophila males, exposing recessive variation that a second chromosome would mask, but 2L, 3L and 3R are ordinary autosomes. Despite the two yeast levels altering lifespan substantially, the distribution of contributing loci is nearly unchanged. Splitting the ten replicates of each treatment into the five odd and the five even gives two independent estimates of every tile's heritability, and hence a pure error term, against which the heritability at each tile can be partitioned into a component shared by all four groups and components differing by sex or by diet (Fig. 1c). Over the significant tiles 6.5% (95% CI 4.2-8.6) of the variation among the four groups is sex-specific and 0.7% (0.3-1.1) diet-specific, with no detectable interaction (-0.1%, -0.3 to 0.1); restricting the partitioning to autosomes the sex-specific fraction remains 5.5% (3.0-7.5). This fraction is invisible to any analysis that pools sexes and treats environmental variables as covariates, as would be the case in human genetics where the environment cannot typically be manipulated and can vary geographically with genotype. Although longevity in Drosophila under two sugar diets is only a single example, if this observation is general it would seem to have implications for polygenic risk scores.
