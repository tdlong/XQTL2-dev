Architecture differs between the sexes but is largely invariant to diet. Of the 268 one cM tiles, 98 reach a -log10 P of 7.5 in at least one group. Male heritability exceeds female at every one of the 15 significant tiles on the X. On the autosomes neither sex is systematically higher, males exceeding females at 52% of 83 tiles. This is perhaps of interest as the X chromosome is hemizygous in Drosophila males. We split the ten replicate contrasts per treatment into sets of five to obtain two independent estimates of heritability at each tile and partitioned the heritability into a component shared by all four groups, components differing by sex or by diet (Fig. 1c), after subtracting the pure error componet. Over the significant tiles 15.3% (95% CI 10.2-24.8) of the variation among the four groups is sex-specific and 1.6% (0.7-3.7) diet-specific, with no detectable interaction (-0.4%, -0.8 to 0.2); over the autosomes alone the sex-specific fraction is 12.8% (8.0-19.1). But it is apparent from Fig. 1c that the relative partitioning of variation varies as a function of location. This genotype by environmental interaction would be refractory to analyses that treat sex and/or environmental variables as covariates removed from phenotypic values before estimating polygenic effects. This would typically be the case in human genetics where the environment is not manipulated and can vary geographically as does genotype. Although longevity in Drosophila under two sugar diets is only a single example, if this observation is general it may have implications for polygenic risk scores.
